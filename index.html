Engagements

Five ways to engage, sized to where the case is.

Scope and posture are confirmed in writing before analysis starts. A screening can be re-cut later. A full analysis framed for the wrong side cannot. See the process and timeline for turnaround expectations on each.

01

Case screening

A short merits memo: what the records show, whether there is a colorable nursing-scope concern, and a straightforward recommendation to proceed, investigate further, decline, or refer.

02

Medical chronology

A chronological care narrative with every entry cited to the Bates page, ready to hand to your retained experts or to use in deposition preparation. Testimony and late-produced records are folded in as they surface.

03

Nursing standard-of-care review

Each nursing finding classified as compliance, potential deviation, or mixed, with the applicable standard cited by organization and edition. Physician-level questions are framed for the MD expert, not answered by the nurse.

04

Full case analysis

Scope of review, chronology, nursing standard of care, causation along both tracks, damages and mitigation as documented, and a flags list: questions for experts, missing records, and discovery topics.

05

Expert report and testimony

A Rule 26(a)(2)(B)-shaped disclosure with numbered opinions, bases, methodology, and exhibits, followed by deposition or trial testimony to a reasonable degree of nursing certainty.

Also available: day-one records-request lists by fact pattern (nurse-call logs, bed-alarm and device data, EHR audit trails, medication administration records, staffing, and policies by title), literature searches, and review of an opposing nurse expert's report.

    The written work product for the scope you chose, followed by a call to walk through what is strong, what is weak, and what is still needed.

How I work

Written to hold up on cross-examination.

Every assertion cites the record

A fact that rests only on a summary, a party-prepared chronology, or a prior report is flagged until it is confirmed against the stamped page.

Nursing scope only

I opine on the nursing standard of care. Physician standard of care and physician-level causation go to the MD expert as framed questions. No legal conclusions.

Both sides of the chart

Compliance findings and potential deviations are both recorded. Emphasis follows the engagement; the findings themselves do not change.

No advocacy in the report

The disclosed report states findings without argument. Framing, emphasis, and rebuttal evidence go to counsel separately as work product.

A clean scope of review

Every document reviewed is listed with its Bates range and date span. Duplicates, exclusions, and missing records are named, not assumed.

HIPAA before records

No protected health information changes hands until a Business Associate Agreement is signed. Conflicts are checked before any records are exchanged.

Questions counsel ask

Straight answers before you retain.

Do you work for plaintiffs or the defense?

Both. The findings are the same either way; only the emphasis follows the engagement. That is what lets the work hold up when the other side reads it.

I already have a physician expert. Why a nurse?

The physician opines on medical judgment. Whether the nursing staff assessed, monitored, escalated, documented, and carried out orders as the nursing standard requires is a nursing question, and a physician is not the witness for it. See the five engagement options for how to add nursing expertise to your case.

Are you still working in the hospital?

Not at the bedside. I am retiring from the service executive role over critical care, progressive care, ortho-neuro, and inpatient wound care at a large community hospital, after nearly four decades there, and legal nurse consulting is where that experience goes next. My Nebraska RN license stays active, I keep current with the standards through AACN, ANA, and AONL, and the standards I cite are the ones I was accountable for on those units.

Which electronic record systems do you know?

Cerner and Epic, including the flowsheets, medication administration records, and audit trails that decide timing disputes the narrative note cannot. I will tell you on day one which system logs to request.

What if the case is outside your areas?

I say so at the first call. Obstetrics, pediatrics, psychiatry, and the operating room are not my areas, and I would rather point you to a specialty-aligned nurse than stretch.

How long does a review take?

A screening usually comes back within two weeks. A chronology or full analysis typically takes three to four weeks depending on the size of the production. Rush work is delivered in ten business days or less at 1.5 times the normal fee. I confirm at the first call that I have room for your deadline before either of us commits.

How many matters do you take?

A few at a time, on purpose. This is a small practice, not a firm, and the person who reads the records is the person who signs the report and sits for the deposition. If I do not have room for your deadline I will say so at the first call and, where I can, name someone who does.

Where do you work?

Records review is remote, for any jurisdiction. For deposition and trial I travel within Nebraska and the neighboring states: Iowa, Missouri, Kansas, Colorado, Wyoming, and South Dakota. Travel time and expenses are agreed in the engagement letter.

How are records handled?

Secure electronic transfer after the Business Associate Agreement is signed, never unencrypted email. Records stay inside the engagement, and at close they are retained or destroyed as the engagement letter provides.

Consulting or testifying?

Either, and the designation is confirmed in writing at the start because it changes the deliverable and what is discoverable. Consulting work stays work product for counsel. An expert report is written to be disclosed under Rule 26 and read by the other side.

What do the credentials mean?

MSN is a master's degree in nursing. NE-BC is board certification as a nurse executive from the American Nurses Credentialing Center. CCRN-K is the critical care credential from the American Association of Critical-Care Nurses for nurses who lead and shape critical care rather than staff a bedside assignment.
